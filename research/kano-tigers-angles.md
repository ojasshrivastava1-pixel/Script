## 1. Hook: "Why did Japan draw tigers like this?"

**Verdict:** ✅

**What to show.** Put a real tiger photo next to a Kano tiger. The differences people see straight away:
- a round, cat-like face
- **slit pupils** (real tigers have **round** pupils; house cats' pupils narrow to slits in bright light)
- small ears and nose
- oversized eyes and paws
- sometimes a spotted animal in the same family scene

**Facts you can lean on**
- The Spencer Museum's label for Kano Tsunenobu's *Tiger and Bamboo* names exactly these traits: relatively small nose and ears, oversized eyes and paws. **[solid]**
- The Smithsonian notes the slit pupils in Ōkyo's *Sitting Tiger* (1777), "an ocular feature common to house cats … but not to tigers". **[solid]**

**Watch out.** "Japan drew tigers like this" is fine as a hook. In the body, say it's centuries of Japanese painters, with the Kano school as the main example.

**Visuals (rights-friendly)**
- Met Open Access and Cleveland Museum of Art Open Access (both CC0 for public-domain works)
- MFA Boston *Tiger and Dragon*, Nelson-Atkins Tan'yū *Dragon and Tiger*, Spencer Tsunenobu: check each museum's image terms

## 2. Personal entry: standing in Nijō Castle, noticing something off

**Verdict:** ⚠️ The angle works. Three practical facts change how you stage it.

**Facts**
- **Where you stood:** the **Tozamurai**, the first and largest building of the Ninomaru Palace. Visiting lords waited there. Its "Tiger Rooms" have gold-ground murals of tigers in bamboo groves, meant to show the shogun's authority. **[solid]** (Nijō Castle official site)
- The third room, nearest the entrance, has **13 tigers and leopards** across three walls: roaring, sleeping, cubs playing. **[solid]** (Kyoto City / Nijō Castle)
- **The panels you saw are almost certainly reproductions.** Since **1972** Nijō has been replacing the palace murals with replicas to protect the originals. The originals (over 1,000 panels, Important Cultural Properties) are kept in the **Nijō-jo Castle Painting Gallery**, opened 2005. It shows about 30 at a time, changing roughly every quarter. **[solid]** (official site)
- **No photos or video inside the Ninomaru or Honmaru palaces.** Commercial or published photography needs a permission application, and approval isn't guaranteed. No drones on the grounds. **[solid]** (official visitor rules)

**How to use it**
- The replica fact can be a small, honest beat: "even the tigers I was looking at were copies, which turns out to fit this story". Copies of copies is the theme of angle 7.
- For footage of the moment, use exterior shots of the castle (allowed on the outdoor route), then cut to museum images of Kano tigers. Or apply to Nijō for permission. Or plan a visit to the Painting Gallery when tiger panels are on show; check the schedule and whether filming is allowed there.

**Open**
- Check whether the Painting Gallery has a Tozamurai tiger rotation coming up (schedule PDFs on the official site).

## 3. Skill paradox: these are gifted artists, so the weirdness isn't incompetence

**Verdict:** ✅ Strong, and you can prove it.

**Facts**
- **Kano Tan'yū** became official painter to the Tokugawa shogunate around **1617, at about 15**. **[solid]**
- In **1626** he led the team painting Nijō's Ninomaru Palace for Emperor Go-Mizunoo's visit, in his early-to-mid twenties. Sources say "about 20" and "about 25"; born 1602, he was about 24. **[solid]**
- The Kano school was the shogunate's official painting establishment for about 250 years of the Edo period. It trained painters by copying masterworks (*funpon*) under a master's critique. **[solid]**
- **When they could look, they got it right.** The British Museum notes that by Tan'yū's time, Kano artists "were being given the task of painting from life, as a record for posterity, pictures of unusual animals and plants". Tan'yū's own bird sketches were copied by followers as models for generations. Kyoto National Museum holds a scroll of his **hawk** sketches. **[solid]**

**The line this gives you:** the same people who could draw a hawk you'd recognise drew a tiger you wouldn't. The difference wasn't skill. It was whether they could see the animal.

**Open**
- A strong visual would be a Tan'yū bird or hawk sketch next to a Tan'yū tiger. Check Kyoto National Museum and British Museum image terms.
- **[check]** The Nijō Ōhiroma's pine-and-hawk murals by Tan'yū would make the same point inside the same building. Confirm before naming them.

## 4. The reveal: the tigers look like another animal in a costume

**Verdict:** ✅ It's more literal than the reel probably meant.

**Facts**
- Painters worked from **imported tiger pelts**, so the stripes and fur came from a real tiger skin. **[solid]**
- The body, face and behaviour came from **house cats**, the only living cat they could watch. Museums describe the result as an "oversized domestic cat". **[solid]**
- **For Tan'yū specifically:** his models "may have been" the cats kept by his patron **Sakuma Shōgen Sanekatsu**. Tan'yū painted a double portrait of Sakuma with his favourite cat. This is Felice Fischer, Philadelphia Museum of Art. **[check]**: she presents it as a possibility, so script it as "one curator thinks…".
- The pelt shaped the body too. On a Buson tiger (1767), the Fukuda Art Museum suggests the huge paws come from a skin **whose paw ends had been cut open** and spread flat. **[likely]**

**The line:** it isn't just like a cat in a costume. That's roughly how it was made: a house cat's body wearing a tiger skin.

**Visuals:** a cat photo, a pelt or rug, and the painting side by side, with the pupils and paws called out.

## 5. Context: Kano school, 17th century

**Verdict:** ⚠️ Fine. Tighten the dates.

**Facts**
- The Kano school ran from the late 1400s to 1868. The 17th century is its peak of official power, when Tan'yū moved its centre to Edo as the shogunate's painters. **[solid]**
- The family line was Masanobu (founder) → Motonobu → Eitoku, who painted for Nobunaga and Hideyoshi → Tan'yū (Eitoku's grandson). **[solid]**
- **Nijō tiger rooms:** painted in **1626** by Kano painters as part of the Ninomaru project led by Tan'yū. Kyoto City attributes the Tozamurai's *Chokushi-no-ma* paintings to **Kano Jinnōjō** (1583–1628). Who painted the tiger rooms is not settled. **[check]**
- Other Kano tigers to cut to:
  - Tan'yū's ~40-panel tiger room at **Nanzen-ji**, including *Tiger Drinking Water*
  - Nagoya Castle's tiger-and-leopard entrance rooms (c. 1615)
  - Kano Sanraku's dragon and tiger screens at Myōshin-ji
  - Kano Tsunenobu's *Tiger and Bamboo*

**Script-safe phrasing:** "painted in 1626 by artists of the Kano school, the shogun's official painters". Don't name a single painter for the Nijō tigers.

## 6. Root cause: Japan has no native tigers, so nobody had seen a living one

**Verdict:** ⚠️ The cause is right. Soften "nobody".

**Facts**
- There were never wild tigers in Japan. **[solid]**
- A handful of live big cats did arrive, mostly as diplomatic curiosities:
  - **1594:** Korea sent **Toyotomi Hideyoshi a live tiger** during peace overtures after his 1592 invasion (Chaiklin, *The Merchant's Ark*). **[likely]**
  - **1860:** an animal shown at Ryōgoku in Edo, billed as a tiger, caused a sensation. MFA Boston says it was **a leopard the Japanese took for a kind of tiger**. A print of it by Yoshitoyo is literally titled "Exhibiting a **leopard** in Western Ryōgoku". **[likely, sources conflict]**
  - **1887:** the first foreign animal shown at **Ueno Zoo** in Tokyo was **a tiger, obtained from Chiarini's Italian circus** (Sainsbury Institute). **[likely]**
- Kyoto National Museum: until the end of the Edo period, for most Japanese the tiger was "a creature from pictures and stories". **[solid]**
- There's no evidence any Kano painter of the Nijō, Nanzen-ji or Nagoya tigers ever saw a live one. **[solid as an absence]**

**Script-safe phrasing:** "Tigers never lived in Japan. Almost nobody there had ever seen one, and certainly not the painters in this room."

## 7. Where the information came from: "painting a rumour"

**Verdict:** ⚠️ The ingredients are right, but "descriptions" is the wrong word. They worked from *pictures*.

**The four sources, by weight**
1. **Chinese paintings.** The tiger came to Japan as a painting subject, especially through Zen ink painting. A Southern Song tiger and dragon pair attributed to **Muqi** at Daitoku-ji, Kyoto, was a standard model. The Met says Japanese artists "seem to have adhered to the initial Chinese models". **[solid]**
2. **Korean paintings.** The Cleveland Museum of Art says Sesson Shūkei's tiger (16th c., not Kano) "is likely modeled after Korean prototypes of the Joseon period circulating in Japan". **[solid]** for that work. A direct Korean-to-Kano link isn't documented yet. **[check]**
3. **Imported pelts:** the stripes, fur and flat-rug proportions. **[solid]**
4. **House cats:** the living anatomy. **[solid]**

**The best evidence for "copies of copies"**
- The Met found a tiger with **a body contour identical to Tan'yū's tiger at Nanzen-ji**. The same tiger appears in Tan'yū's ***shukuzu***, his miniature reference copies of old Asian paintings. **[solid]** So a Kano tiger could be a copy of a copy of a Chinese or Korean original, with no live animal anywhere in the chain.

**Two primary quotes**
- **Itō Jakuchū, 1755** (not Kano, same problem): "Because there are no ferocious tigers in Japan, I have imitated the painting of [the Southern Song artist] Mao Yi." **[solid]**
- **1860:** a pamphlet by Kanagaki Robun, illustrated by Yoshitoyo, sold to the crowds: *An Easy-to-Understand Explanation of the Fierce Tiger Imported from the Netherlands* (Edo-Tokyo Museum). **[likely]** It shows how badly people wanted real information. Worth a look as a visual.

**Fixed phrasing:** "They painted from Chinese and Korean paintings, from tiger skins, and from the cat on the veranda. They were painting a rumour, passed from picture to picture."

**Open**
- A written-description angle could work through Chinese texts (e.g. the *Bencao gangmu* tiger entry, or the 1712 Japanese encyclopedia *Wakan sansai zue*). This research didn't confirm what those entries say.

## 8. Confusion in the source: leopards, male and female

**Verdict:** ❌ **The reel has it backwards.** Fix this before scripting.

- **Wrong:** "male and female tigers treated as different animals".
- **Right:** **two different animals were treated as the male and female of one.** The striped tiger was the male; the spotted **leopard** (*hyō*) was believed to be the **female tiger**. **[solid]** (Smithsonian, Timon Screech's *Obtaining Images* p. 36, Yamanashi Prefectural Museum, Hayashibara Museum)

**Facts**
- Fischer, on the Kano tigers: there was "some confusion about 'spotted' tigers", and "often the smaller tigers, whether meant to be female or cubs, are spotted". **[solid]**
- A Nijō guide explains the Tozamurai's mix of tigers and leopards the same way: leopards were thought to be female tigers. **[likely]**
- The belief outlived the Kano heyday. A Nagasaki University doctoral thesis (Sakamoto) on tigers and leopards imported into early modern Japan focuses on the leopard **"recognised as the same species as the tiger"**. **[likely]**
- It fed straight into 1860: the Ryōgoku animal was called a tiger, a leopard, and "tiger-leopard" in different prints. **[likely]**

**The line:** "In the shogun's tiger room, some of the tigers have spots. They aren't mistakes. They're the wives, or so the painters believed."

**Open**
- Find where the belief came from: a Chinese text, or a Japanese inference from pelts arriving in pairs? Not established.

## 9. Twist: not bad paintings, good paintings of a wrong idea

**Verdict:** ✅

**Facts and support**
- The tiger was a **symbol**, not a zoological subject:
  - With the dragon, it stood for **yin and yang**: "the tiger's roar raises the wind, the dragon's rise brings the clouds".
  - In castles, tigers projected the lord's power and intimidated visitors. **[solid]**
- **The dragon comparison:** the tiger was usually paired with a dragon, and for Japanese viewers both were equally unseen. **[solid]**
- **The lion comparison:** the same painters made lions (*karajishi*) that look nothing like lions, because Japanese artists "never saw the real beast" (JAANUS). Kano Eitoku's *Karajishi* screen (Imperial Collection) is a masterpiece, and nobody calls it a bad lion. **[solid]** The tiger got laughed at because it sits between myth and zoo animal.
- **The craft holds up:** gold-leaf grounds, ink control, composition at architectural scale. They were successful as what they were meant to be: images of authority. **[solid as general consensus]**

**The line:** "These aren't bad paintings of a tiger. They're excellent paintings of what a tiger was believed to be."

## 10. Closing: what artists draw depends on the information they have

**Verdict:** ✅ You called it the least certain. It's actually the best supported, and you can show it three ways.

1. **The artists said so.** Jakuchū's inscription states it outright: no tigers here, so I copied a painting. **[solid]**
2. **When information improved, the tigers changed.**
   - Kishi Ganku (Kyoto, about 1800) worked from **a tiger skull and skins imported from China**, and his tigers are noticeably more anatomical. **[likely]**
   - **1860:** live big cats in Edo, with crowds, prints and explainer pamphlets.
   - **1887:** a tiger at Ueno Zoo. The information arrived, and the picture changed with it. **[likely]**
3. **It happened elsewhere too: Dürer's *Rhinoceros* (1515).** Dürer never saw the animal. He worked from a written description and a sketch of a rhino sent to Lisbon, and gave it plate armour and an extra horn on its back. His image became Europe's standard rhinoceros, copied by naturalists such as Gessner, until the **18th century**. **[solid]** (NHM London, NGA, Wikipedia)

**Optional final beat:** the tigers in the room were also copies, of copies, of a rumour. Your angle 2 detail comes back at the end.

**Watch out.** The "same mistake everywhere" idea is a broadening. Keep it to one example (Dürer) so the piece stays about the tigers.
