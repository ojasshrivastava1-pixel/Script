# Kano School Tigers: Research Brief

Working context for a deep dive on why Japanese painters of the Kano school (and their neighbours) painted tigers that look so strange: cat faces, giant paws, spotted "wives".

Confidence tags used below:
- **[solid]**: several independent or authoritative sources (museum, scholarly) agree
- **[likely]**: one good source, or several weaker ones
- **[check]**: single, commercial, or conflicting source. Verify before it goes in a script.

---

## 1. The crux in one paragraph

Tigers never lived in Japan. For centuries, the painters who made the most prestigious images in the country, the Kano school, painted tigers for shoguns' castles and Zen temples without ever having seen one. They built the animal from three secondhand ingredients: **Chinese paintings** (copied and recopied through the Kano workshop's model books), **imported tiger pelts** (flat, stretched, with cut-open paws), and **house cats** (the only living feline to hand). The result is a "tiger" with a cat's round face and slit pupils, oversized paws, small ears, and sometimes a spotted mate, because painters believed the **leopard was the female tiger**. These were not failed attempts at realism. The tiger was a symbolic creature, just as imaginary to its Japanese viewers as the dragon it was usually paired with.

---

## 2. Who the Kano school were

- Japan's dominant hereditary painting dynasty, from the late 1400s to the end of the Edo period (1868). Japanese sources often say "about 400 years". The Met says "more than 300 years" of prominence. **[solid]**
- **Kano Masanobu** (1434–1530) founded it as a professional atelier in Kyoto. **[solid]**
- **Kano Motonobu** (1476–1559) merged Chinese-style ink painting with Japanese decorative taste and built the studio system that trained generation after generation. **[solid]**
- **Kano Eitoku** (1543–1590), Motonobu's grandson, painted the vast gold-ground murals of Oda Nobunaga's and Toyotomi Hideyoshi's castles. Few of his works survive. **[solid]**
- **Kano Sanraku** (1559–1635) headed the Kyoto line (Kyō-Kanō). **[solid]**
- **Kano Tan'yū** (1602–1674), Eitoku's grandson, became official painter to the Tokugawa shogunate in 1617, aged about 15, and moved the school's centre to Edo. **[solid]**
- In the Edo period the school became a bureaucracy: four "inner" painter families (*oku eshi*: Kajibashi, Nakabashi, Kobikichō, Hamachō) plus about sixteen "outer" branches (*omote eshi*). **[solid]**
- **Training was copying.** Students spent their days copying model images (*funpon*) under a master's critique. Tan'yū also kept *shukuzu*, miniature reference copies of old paintings brought to him for appraisal. **[solid]** This matters for the tigers (see 3d).

## 3. Why the tigers look weird: the causes

### 3a. Nobody had seen a tiger
- Tigers are not native to Japan, and there were never wild tigers there. **[solid]**
- Live tigers almost never reached Japan. A documented case: in **1594 Korea sent Toyotomi Hideyoshi a live tiger** during attempts to open peace talks after his 1592 invasion (Chaiklin, *The Merchant's Ark*). **[likely]**
- Even Kyoto National Museum's own explanation says that until the end of the Edo period, for most Japanese the tiger was "a creature from pictures and stories that could not actually be seen". **[solid]**
- The best-known public showing: in **1860**, at a show (*misemono*) in Ryōgoku, Edo, an animal billed as a tiger drew crowds and inspired prints, among them Kawanabe Kyōsai's *Never Seen Before: True Picture of a Live Wild Tiger*. **But** MFA Boston says the animal was **an imported leopard that the Japanese thought was a kind of tiger**. Another print is titled "Exhibiting a leopard in Western Ryōgoku", and Hiroshige II titled his "Tiger Leopard at Ryōgoku". **[likely]** So the "first live tiger" many Edo people saw may have been a leopard.

### 3b. Pelts: the shape comes from a rug
- Most painters used **imported tiger skins** as reference. **[solid]**
- Working from a flat hide distorts proportions. Museums list **small noses and ears, oversized eyes, and enormous paws** (Spencer Museum label for Kano Tsunenobu's *Tiger and Bamboo*). **[solid]**
- On Yosa Buson's 1767 tiger, the Fukuda Art Museum suggests the paws are huge because the painter worked from a pelt **whose paw ends had been cut open** and splayed. **[likely]**
- The coat could look convincing (the stripes were right there on the hide) while the body and character came out like an oversized house cat (Freiburg museum, on Tachibana Morikuni's 1770 pattern book). **[likely]**

### 3c. Cats: the living model
- Several sources say painters **used house cats as live models**. **[solid]** as a general practice.
- **Telltale sign: slit pupils.** Big cats like tigers have **round** pupils. Domestic cats' pupils narrow to vertical slits in bright light. Maruyama Ōkyo's *Sitting Tiger* (1777) has slit pupils (Smithsonian). **[solid]** on the biology.
- **For Tan'yū specifically:** Felice Fischer (Philadelphia Museum of Art) suggests his models "may have been" the cats kept by one of his patrons, **Sakuma Shōgen Sanekatsu**. Tan'yū also painted a double portrait of Sakuma with his favourite cat. **[check]**: Fischer herself frames it as speculation.

### 3d. Chinese models, copied through the Kano machine
- The tiger came to Japan as a **Chinese painting subject**, especially through Chan/Zen ink painting. A famous pair of Southern Song tiger and dragon scrolls attributed to **Muqi** (mid-1200s) at **Daitoku-ji, Kyoto**, became a standard model. Later artists made versions of it (e.g. Denver Art Museum's pair). **[likely]**
- The Met says Japanese artists "seem to have adhered to the initial Chinese models". It also says a tiger in the Met's (Chinese/Korean/Japanese, attribution unsettled) painting has **a body contour identical to Kano Tan'yū's tiger at Nanzen-ji**, and that the same tiger appears in Tan'yū's *shukuzu* of old Asian paintings. **[solid]**: this is the strongest evidence for the "template" idea.
- **Working hypothesis for the script:** Kano training meant a tiger was learned from a model book, not from nature. An anatomical oddity in a revered Chinese original could be copied, canonized and passed down for generations. **(Our interpretation. Phrase it as one.)**
- A parallel outside the Kano school: Itō Jakuchū's *Tiger* (1755) carries his own inscription, "Because there are no ferocious tigers in Japan, I have imitated the painting of [the Southern Song artist] Mao Yi." **[solid]** The best primary-source quote for the whole topic.

### 3e. The leopard is the tiger's wife
- In premodern Japan the **leopard (*hyō*) was widely believed to be the female tiger**: striped male, spotted female. **[solid]** (Smithsonian, Timon Screech's *Obtaining Images* p. 36, Yamanashi Prefectural Museum, Hayashibara Museum).
- So "tiger family" scenes often mix striped and spotted animals. This shows up in the big Kano castle programmes (see Nijō and Nagoya below).

### Adjacent (non-Kano) tigers worth knowing, for contrast and B-roll
- **Maruyama Ōkyo**, *Sitting Tiger* (1777): slit cat pupils. Ōkyo **trained in the Kano lineage** under Ishida Yūtei before founding his own realist school. **[solid]**
- **Nagasawa Rosetsu** (Ōkyo's pupil), *Tiger* fusuma (1786), Muryō-ji, Kushimoto: an Important Cultural Property, legendarily painted in one night. One reading: the back of the panels shows **a cat pouncing on a fish**, so the giant "tiger" is the cat **as seen by the fish**. **[check]**: single web-magazine source (Wa-gokoro / intojapanwaraku).
- **Itō Jakuchū**, *Tiger* (1755): the Mao Yi inscription (see 3d).
- **Kishi Ganku** (Kyoto): famous for tigers, said to have worked from **a tiger skull and skins imported from China**. **[likely]**
- **Ogata Kōrin**, *Bamboo and Tiger*: an openly adorable tiger. It inspired Kyoto National Museum's mascot "Torarin". **[likely]**
- **Tawaraya Sōtatsu** (attrib.), tiger c. 1630–40 (Met): "a gentle, even adorable, feline". **[likely]**
- **Ōtsu-e** folk paintings: cartoon tigers sold to travellers. **[solid]**
- **Korea's *hojak-do*** (magpie and tiger) folk paintings: a deliberately foolish "idiot tiger" satirising the aristocracy. Korea was full of real tigers, so its goofiness is **satire, not ignorance**. A useful contrast with Japan. **[solid]**

## 5. Why paint tigers at all? Symbolism

- **Power theatre.** Tigers went into **castle entrance and reception halls** (Nijō, Nagoya, the original home of the Nanzen-ji panels) to project the lord's authority and intimidate visitors. **[solid]**
- **Dragon and tiger (*ryūko-zu*).** From Chinese cosmology, via Zen: dragon = yang, heaven, clouds and rain; tiger = yin, earth. "The tiger's roar raises the wind, the dragon's rise brings the clouds." This was especially popular with the warrior class. **[solid]**
- **Tiger and bamboo.** A standard pairing. The wind from the tiger's roar bends the bamboo. **[solid]**
- **Prestige of the pelt.** The warrior elite prized tiger skins (draped over armour). Katō Kiyomasa's legendary tiger hunt in Korea (1590s) became a popular print subject. The hunt itself is **[check]**: Wikipedia flags it as disputed, and versions differ.
- **Earliest Japanese tiger story:** the *Nihon Shoki* tale of Kashiwade no Hasuhi killing a tiger in Baekje (Korea), later a Kuniyoshi print. **[likely]**

## 6. Myth vs. fact log (verify before scripting)

| Claim | Verdict so far |
|---|---|
| "Japanese artists used cats as tiger models" | True as a general practice. For Tan'yū specifically it is curator speculation (Sakuma's cats). |
| "They thought leopards were female tigers" | Well supported (Screech, museums). |
| "Tigers in Japanese art have cat pupils" | True for many works (e.g. Ōkyo 1777). Real tigers have round pupils. |
| "Nobody in Japan saw a live tiger before Meiji" | Overstated. Hideyoshi got one in 1594. The 1860 Edo "tiger" may have been a leopard. |
| Nanzen-ji tigers' origin | Conflicting: 1630s castle → Nanzen-ji in the 1650s (Fischer) vs. Fushimi Castle building moved ~1605–11. |
| Nijō tiger-room painter | Conflicting: Kano Jinnōjō vs. Kano Tan'yū. |
| Rosetsu's tiger = cat seen by a fish | One source only. |
| Kano school lasted "400 years" | Roughly the late 1400s to 1868 (~380 yrs). The Met says "300+ years" of prominence. |
| Muqi's Daitoku-ji pair was the Kano template | Plausible and widely repeated. The direct evidence found so far is the Met's Tan'yū contour match, not a Daitoku-ji document. |

## 7. Story angles and hooks

1. **"Painting an animal no one had ever seen."** For Japanese viewers, the tiger was as imaginary as the dragon it was paired with.
2. **The recipe:** Chinese painting + tiger-skin rug + the cat next door = a Kano tiger.
3. **Intimidation that comes out cute:** the shogun's most terrifying reception rooms are guarded by giant house cats.
4. **The copy machine:** a dynasty that trained by copying copies, so a mistake could become canon.
5. **The leopard wives:** the "female tigers" in the castle murals.
6. **The 1860 twist:** when Edo finally queued to see a real tiger, it may have been a leopard.
7. **Primary-source mic drop:** Jakuchū: "Because there are no ferocious tigers in Japan, I have imitated…"
8. **Modern lens:** the internet laughs at "derpy" tigers, but these paintings never aimed at zoology. Contrast with Korea's deliberately silly tigers in a country full of real ones.
