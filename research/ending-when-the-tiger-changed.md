# Ending: The Day the Tiger Changed

When a Japanese painter finally saw a live tiger, what happened to the paintings? This is material for the script's closing section. It pays off angle 10: "what artists draw depends on the information they have."

Tags: **[solid]** well documented · **[likely]** one good source · **[check]** anecdote or single source

---

## The story in four steps

### 1. Around 1800: Kishi Ganku gets closer, but still hasn't seen one
- **Kishi Ganku** founded the Kishi school in Kyoto and became Japan's most famous tiger painter. **[solid]**
- He's said to have worked from **a tiger skull and skins imported from China**. His tigers are fiercer and more anatomical than the Kano tigers, but he never saw a live one. **[likely]**
- Ganku is the step between: better information (bones and skin), still no living animal.

### 2. 1886: Ganku's heir sees a real tiger, at about 60 — **the turning point**
- **Kishi Chikudō** (1826–1897) was the **fourth head of the Kishi school**, a Kyoto master already known for tigers. **[solid]** He trained under **Kano and Shijō teachers** early on. **[likely]** (Honolulu Museum of Art biography)
- In **1886 (Meiji 19)**, the **Chiarini Italian circus** came to Kyoto with **real tigers**. **[likely]** (Shiga Museum of Art)
- Honolulu Museum of Art: although he was an expert, his tigers "**looked like large cats**". When he finally saw a tiger at a travelling circus, "the first time he saw one in real life", he was "**shocked by its appearance, and his painting style changed completely**". **[likely]**
- He reportedly went **day after day** to sketch it. Afterwards his tigers look like real tigers (Shiga Museum). **[likely]**
- **The legend:** he became so obsessed with the tiger that he lost his mind. This comes from an anecdote book, *Gaka itsuji-dan* (1903). A modern essay cites the *Century Magazine* reporting "temporary mental derangement". **[check]**: anecdote. If you use it, say "the story goes".
- His tiger was shown at the **1893 Chicago World's Fair**. **[solid]** (Met) Fair coverage reportedly called it "a marvel of Japanese realism". **[check]**
- **Date caveat:** one account says he was 63 when he first saw the tiger, which would be about 1888–89, not 1886. The circus date of 1886 is solid. Exactly when he saw it is a bit uncertain.

### 3. 1891–92: a cat painter meets a tiger
- **Ōhashi Suiseki** (1865–1945), from Ōgaki in Gifu. **[solid]**
- After the **1891 Nōbi earthquake** (magnitude 8; it killed his father and destroyed his home), a **tiger show was set up in the burned ruins**. He went **every day** and sketched it so thoroughly that people talked about it. **[likely]** (Wikipedia JA, Gifu sources, UAG)
- He had been an animal painter who **painted cats**. A *Kitten* painting by him survives in the Fukuda Art Museum, and one source says cats were his speciality. **[check]** on "speciality". That he painted cats is **[solid]**.
- **1900 Paris World's Fair:** his *Fierce Tiger* won **the only gold medal awarded to a Japanese painter**. More golds followed at St. Louis (1904) and the Japan–British Exhibition (1910). **[solid]** (Gifu Prefectural Museum)
- Gifu Prefectural Museum describes his tigers as having high realism "backed by having actually seen them". **[solid]**

### 4. 1887 onward: the tiger becomes ordinary
- Ueno Zoo's first foreign animal is a tiger, from that same Chiarini circus (1887). **[likely]** From then on, any painter could go and look.

## Why this works as an ending

- **It closes the loop on your own argument.** The skill was always there. When the information changed, the picture changed, almost overnight, in the same family of painters.
- **The 260-year gap:** Nijō's tiger rooms (1626) to Chikudō at the circus (1886). Same brushes, same tradition. The only new thing was a living animal in front of the painter.
- **Two strong images:**
  1. **A master tiger painter, about 60 years old, seeing a tiger for the first time**, and realising his family had been painting cats. He goes back every day.
  2. **The cat painter who sees a tiger in the ruins of an earthquake**, and nine years later wins Paris. It echoes the start, where the tigers were made from house cats.
- **Honest framing:** Chikudō and Suiseki are not Kano painters. Chikudō trained with Kano teachers, but he headed the Kishi school. Say "Japanese painters" for the ending, not "the Kano school".

## Ending beats (a skeleton; write it in your own voice)

1. Back to the room at Nijō: tigers that look like cats.
2. Jump 260 years: Kyoto, 1886, an Italian circus comes to town.
3. Kishi Chikudō, master tiger painter, sees a real tiger for the first time. His tigers change completely.
4. (Optional) Ōhashi Suiseki sketches a tiger in earthquake ruins, and his tiger wins gold in Paris in 1900.
5. The point: the painters didn't get better. They got to look.
6. Final line: the angle 10 idea, that what artists draw depends on what they know.
