# When Did Tigers (and Japanese People) First Meet?

Short answer: it depends what "first" means. Each milestone below is tagged by how well it's documented.

Tags: **[solid]** well documented · **[likely]** one good source · **[check]** secondary or unverified

---

## Timeline

| Year | What happened | Who saw it | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **545** | The *Nihon Shoki* (720) says **Kashiwade no Hasuhi**, a Japanese envoy in **Baekje (Korea)**, killed a tiger that had taken his child and brought back its skin. The earliest tiger in Japanese records. | One Japanese man, **in Korea**. A tiger skin reached Japan. | **[likely]**: a chronicle story with a legendary flavour. Later a Kuniyoshi print subject. |
| **7th c.** | Gifts from Silla (Korea) to the Japanese court reportedly included **tigers and leopards**, probably skins. | The court | **[check]**: Kyushu University paper; live or pelt not confirmed |
| **890** | Said to be the **first live tiger in Japan**: **two tiger cubs from Silla** presented to **Emperor Uda**, who had the court painter **Kose no Kanaoka** sketch them. | The emperor's court, and one painter | **[check]**: only via zoologist Takashima Haruo (1984), cited by Hamada & Lee. No primary chronicle entry found yet. A Nikkei article also says the first arrival is "traditionally" dated 890. |
| **1592–98** | **Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea.** Tens of thousands of Japanese soldiers are in tiger country. Hideyoshi orders his generals to send him **tiger meat and organs**. Tiger hunts become a samurai legend (e.g. Katō Kiyomasa, partly embellished). | **The first time large numbers of Japanese saw live tigers**, but in Korea | **[solid]** |
| **1594** | Korea sends **Hideyoshi a live tiger** during peace overtures. | Hideyoshi's court | **[likely]** (Chaiklin, *The Merchant's Ark*) |
| **1595 (10 Mar.)** | **Shimazu Yoshihiro's** men capture **two tigers** in the mountains near **Changwon**, Korea, on Hideyoshi's orders. | Shimazu troops | **[solid]** (Yamaguchi Makoto, 2016 paper) |
| **1614** | The Dutch reportedly bring **two tiger cubs** as a gift. | Shogunal elite | **[check]**: one listing summarising Laver's research. Verify in Laver, *The Dutch East India Company in Early Modern Japan* (2020). |
| **1626** | **The Kano school paints Nijō Castle's tiger rooms.** Live tigers have reached Japan before this, but there's **no evidence any of these painters saw one**. | — | — |
| **1860** | **The first public show**: an animal billed as a tiger is shown at **Ryōgoku, Edo**, to huge crowds, prints and pamphlets. MFA Boston and some prints say it was **a leopard**. | **The first time ordinary Japanese saw a big cat in Japan** | **[likely]**: the animal's identity is contested |
| **1886** | **Chiarini's Royal Italian Circus** tours Japan with **Indian tigers** in its acts, including a show before Emperor Meiji (1 Nov. 1886). | The public: the first **certain** live tigers for ordinary audiences | **[likely]** |
| **1887** | **Ueno Zoo's first foreign animal is a tiger**, obtained from Chiarini's circus. | The public, permanently | **[likely]** (Sainsbury Institute) |

## What it means for the script

- **"No Japanese person had ever seen a tiger" is wrong.** Japanese soldiers saw plenty in Korea in the 1590s, and rulers were sent live ones as gifts.
- **What is true:** for about a thousand years, the few tigers that came were **diplomatic gifts for emperors and warlords**. They were rare and kept out of sight. Ordinary people didn't get to see a big cat until **1860**, and maybe not a real tiger until the **1886 circus**.
- **Painters were mostly locked out.** The one claimed exception is the **890** story of Kose no Kanaoka sketching Emperor Uda's cubs, which is unverified but would be a great detail if it holds. Otherwise there's no record of the Kano painters seeing a live tiger.
- **Interesting wrinkle (open question):** Kano Sanraku worked for Hideyoshi in the 1590s, when Hideyoshi was receiving tigers. Did any Kano painter see one? No evidence found either way.

**Script-safe line:** "Tigers never lived in Japan. A few arrived over the centuries as gifts for emperors and warlords, but ordinary people, and the painters, almost never saw one. The public didn't see a big cat until 1860."
